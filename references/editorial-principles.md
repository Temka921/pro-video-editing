# Editorial principles

Condensed from the ideas in Walter Murch's *In the Blink of an Eye*. This is a paraphrase for working use; the book is worth reading in full.

## The stance

1. **Most of editing is not cutting.** The work is watching, comparing and rejecting. Many cuts are considered for each one kept. Never measure progress by number of edits.
2. **Emotion decides.** Before each decision ask what the viewer should feel. If they feel it throughout, you have done most of what an editor can do.
3. **Do the most with the least.** Suggest instead of explaining. Past a point, added detail turns viewers from participants into spectators. This holds for cuts, effects, music and sound layers alike. A sound mix is not good because it has many tracks.
4. **Not cutting is a decision.** Holding a shot often takes more judgment than cutting it. Do not cut to look busy. A guide who never lets visitors look for themselves is pursuing control that defeats itself.
5. **Know what this piece is.** The order and timing of information define a piece; the same footage can become very different pieces. Fix the intent early and do not change identity halfway.
6. **Judge only what is on screen.** Ignore how hard a shot was to get, what it cost, or what was meant. The viewer sees only the frame.

## The cut hierarchy

An ideal cut satisfies all six criteria. When it cannot, give up items from the bottom first.

| Priority | Criterion | Test |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Emotion | Does it keep the feeling the moment needs? |
| 2 | Story | Does it move the story forward? |
| 3 | Rhythm | Does it land where it feels right? |
| 4 | Eye-trace | Is the viewer's eye already where the next shot's focus is? |
| 5 | Planarity | Does it respect screen direction in the flat frame? |
| 6 | Spatial continuity | Do positions in 3D space stay consistent? |

Rules of use:

- Never trade a higher item for a lower one. Do not give up emotion before story, story before rhythm, rhythm before eye-trace, eye-trace before planarity, planarity before spatial continuity.
- The top three are tightly bound and carry most of the weight. Getting a higher item right hides flaws lower down; the reverse is not true. A cut with true emotion and a continuity error beats a continuous cut that feels dead.
- **Eye-trace in practice:** note where attention sits at the end of the outgoing shot (a face, a moving object, text). Place the incoming shot's subject in or near that screen region, or move it on purpose when you want a jolt.
- **Misdirection:** an editor directs attention the way a magician does. Decide where the viewer looks, and what they will not notice.
- Never accept less when more is available.

## Finding the cut point

A cut works when it falls where a thought ends. People blink when they finish a thought or grasp an idea. A good cut sits at that same instant.

- **A cut point should be a possible blink point.** Cut where the viewer has just absorbed the idea, or a hair before. Not every cut needs a blink; it needs to be able to take one.
- Blinks and cuts follow thought, not the other way round. Placing a cut does not create the moment. Find the moment, then cut.
- A committed performer blinks where the character's thought turns. Use on-screen blinks, breaths, glances and shifts of posture as markers.
- Give the audience what they need just before they would ask for it. Aim to be surprising and self-evident at once.

Signals to use when working from data and not by eye:

- **Transcript:** end of a clause or sentence, the beat after a key word, a breath, a pause
- **Picture:** a blink, a look away, completion of a gesture or movement
- **Audio:** a dip in energy, the tail of a sound

Choose the point by playing through in real time and marking where it feels right, then repeat. If the mark lands on the same frame each time, that is the cut. If it drifts, the moment is not found yet.

## Dialogue and pacing

- Do not cut mechanically to whoever is speaking with each line shown in full. That pattern is simple and reads as flat. Real conversation has a grammar of looks, reactions and overlaps.
- Cut on the listener's understanding. Let lines overlap picture changes so reactions carry meaning.
- Staying on a speaker past the end of a line, or leaving before it ends, tells the viewer what to think about that person.
- Cut rate follows thought rate: more cuts for conflict and action, fewer for calm or reflective material.
- Long holds create objectivity and let viewers choose what to look at. Use them on purpose.
- Vary rhythm. Uniform shot lengths read as machine-made.

## Logging and selecting takes

- Watch or index all the footage, not only the obvious takes.
- For each setup pick one **representative frame**: the instant that carries the shot's emotion. Describe it in words. It usually ends up in the cut, near a cut point.
- Treat each take as an audition. Note what each offers that the others do not. Resist the dulling effect of seeing many takes in a row.
- Write notes against the original source clips, never against proxies.
- After the first full pass, **go back through the rejected footage**. The piece has changed, and shots that looked unusable may now be right.
- Removing something you thought essential can free the piece. If its job is already done elsewhere, cut it.

## Reviewing and diagnosing

- Watch the whole piece straight through at full size without stopping, as if you could not change it.
- **Referred pain.** Where a viewer says the problem is often is not where it is. Confusion in one scene is usually caused by something missing or misplaced earlier. Do not operate on the place that hurts until you have traced the cause.
- Feedback shows where the piece stands, not how to fix it. Considered reactions a day or two later are more reliable than instant ones.
- Do not get lost in frame-level detail and lose the overall shape. Alternate close work with full watch-throughs.
- If you have lost objectivity, step away, then rewatch as a stranger.
- Changes made close to delivery are permanent. Be most careful then.

## Working with the person

- They hold the vision; your job is to draw it out. **Offer concrete alternatives** (a different order, a different take, a scene removed). Reactions to a real option reveal what they want faster than questions do.
- Present changes as a reviewable list (what changed, where, why) before rendering anything expensive.
- When several people or agents edit one piece, speed goes up and coherence is at risk. Agree on intent and style first, and have one pass unify the result.
- Push back when a request trades emotion or story for something lower on the hierarchy, then do what they decide.
